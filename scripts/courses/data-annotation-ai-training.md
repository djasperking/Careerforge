## 👋 Welcome: your path into AI work

Behind every AI tool you use (chatbots, voice assistants, photo apps, translation) are **people** who taught it. That teaching is called **data annotation** or **AI training**, and much of it can be done remotely, by beginners, with a computer and careful attention.

### What you will do in this course

- 🤖 Learn how AI learns from people
- 🏷️ Practise real task types with **scored drills**, the same style companies use to test annotators
- 📘 Learn to follow **guidelines** exactly
- 🔍 Handle **edge cases**, fact-checking and ranking
- 🔒 Spot **job scams** and apply safely
- 🧪 Finish with a practical **Annotator Readiness Project**

> 📌 **Remember:** AI learns from examples that humans prepare, and you can be one of those humans.

::: check Who prepares much of the data that AI learns from?
- Nobody; AI learns by itself
+ People who label, rank and review examples
- Only the engineers who build it
= Humans give AI the examples and the feedback it learns from.
:::

### An honest note

Annotation work can be a good way to earn from home, but:

- **Pay, hours and availability vary.** Nobody can promise a fixed income.
- Many people are **tested first**, and not everyone passes the first time.
- **Projects start and end**, so work can be uneven.
- You should **never pay money to get a job**. We will show you how to spot scams.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** if someone promises guaranteed income for a fee, walk away.

::: check Should you pay a fee to be given an annotation job?
- Yes, to secure your place
+ No. Real employers do not charge you to work
- Only if the fee is small
= Asking for money up front is the number one scam sign.
:::

## 🧠 How AI learns from people

### The simple pipeline

1. **Collect data:** text, images, audio, video.
2. **People label it:** each example gets the right answer (a label).
3. **The model trains:** it studies thousands or millions of labelled examples and finds patterns.
4. **People test it:** they check whether the answers are right.
5. **People give feedback:** they compare and rank answers so the model improves.

> **Example:** to teach a model to spot spam, people label thousands of messages as "spam" or "not spam". The model learns what spam usually looks like.

> 📌 **Remember:** the quality of the labels decides the quality of the AI. **Bad labels in, bad AI out.**

::: check What happens if the labels are wrong or inconsistent?
+ The model learns the wrong patterns
- The model fixes them by itself
- Nothing
= Garbage in, garbage out.
:::

### Chatbots and human feedback

Many chatbots are improved by people who **read two answers and choose the better one**, and who **write ideal answers** for the model to learn from. This is why careful, honest, clear judgment matters.

### Why companies hire people

Machines need **human judgment** for things like sarcasm, safety, cultural meaning, local language and "is this actually correct?". Companies need **large numbers of careful people**, and they pay for accuracy.

::: check Why do companies need human annotators?
+ Machines need human judgment on meaning, safety and correctness
- Because computers cannot be switched on
- Only to save paper
= People supply the judgment models are trained on.
:::

## 🏷️ The main types of annotation work

### The task types

| Type | What you do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Classification** | Put an item in a category | Is this review positive, negative or neutral? |
| **Bounding boxes and tagging** | Mark things in images | Draw a box around every car |
| **Transcription** | Type what is said or written | Turn an audio clip into text |
| **Named entities** | Highlight names, places, dates | Mark "Lagos" as a place |
| **Ranking and comparison** | Choose the better output | Which of two AI answers is better, and why? |
| **Writing** | Create examples | Write a question and an ideal answer |
| **Review and moderation** | Check content for quality and safety | Does this follow the rules? |

> 📌 **Remember:** each task has a **guideline**, a document that explains exactly how to do it for that project.

::: check Which is a ranking task?
- Typing out an audio recording
+ Choosing which of two AI answers is better
- Drawing a box around a car
= Ranking means comparing outputs and judging quality.
:::

### A day in the work

1. Open the project and **re-read the guideline**.
2. Do a **batch of tasks** with full attention.
3. Use the **flag or unsure** option when needed.
4. **Take breaks.** Fresh eyes make fewer mistakes.
5. Your **quality** is checked, and tasks continue if it is good.

### What good annotators have

- **Careful reading**
- **Consistency**
- **Honesty about not knowing**
- **Reliability** and focus

::: check Which habit matters most for quality?
+ Reading the guideline carefully and staying consistent
- Working as fast as possible
- Guessing when unsure
= Accuracy and consistency drive quality scores.
:::

## 📘 Drill 1: apply the guideline

### The guideline is the boss

A guideline usually contains: the **goal**, **definitions**, **examples**, **edge cases** and **what to do when unsure**. Annotators who read it fully, and again when stuck, score higher than fast readers.

> 📌 **Remember:** apply the **rule**, not your opinion.

::: check A guideline says "label sarcasm as negative". You find it funny. What do you do?
- Label it funny
+ Follow the guideline and label it negative
- Skip the task
= Annotators apply the project's rules, not personal taste.
:::

### The drill

Companies check your work with **gold tasks**: hidden questions with known answers. This drill works the same way. You get **instant feedback**, and need **75%** to pass.

::: drill Toxic or not toxic
labels: Toxic | Not toxic
rule: Label TOXIC only if the message insults a person or a group. Strong opinions, complaints about things, and criticism of ideas are NOT toxic.
pass: 75
- You are an idiot and nobody likes you. => Toxic | It insults a person.
- This weather is rubbish! => Not toxic | It complains about a thing, not a person.
- I disagree with the new price, it is too high. => Not toxic | It criticises a price, with no insult.
- People from that town are all thieves. => Toxic | It insults a whole group.
- That film was the worst I have ever seen. => Not toxic | A strong opinion about a thing.
- Shut up, you stupid fool. => Toxic | It insults a person.
- Your argument has a big mistake in the second step. => Not toxic | It criticises an idea, not the person.
- Honestly the app crashes every day, it is useless. => Not toxic | It complains about the app.
:::

> 📌 **Remember:** read the rule first, then decide. If you missed some, notice which part of the rule you skipped.

## 😀 Drill 2: sentiment and the Mixed label

### Sentiment classification

One of the most common tasks: **what feeling does the writer express?**

- **Positive:** clear good feeling.
- **Negative:** clear bad feeling.
- **Neutral:** facts with no feeling.
- **Mixed:** clear positive and clear negative in the same message.

> 📌 **Remember:** facts are **Neutral**, even if the fact is good news, unless the writer shows feeling.

::: check "The package weighs 2 kilograms." is...
- Positive
- Negative
+ Neutral
= It states a fact with no feeling.
:::

### The drill

::: drill Sentiment
labels: Positive | Negative | Neutral | Mixed
rule: Label the feeling the writer expresses. Facts with no feeling are Neutral. Use Mixed only when there is a clear positive AND a clear negative in the same message.
pass: 70
- The delivery arrived on time and the food was hot. => Positive | It praises the delivery.
- I waited two hours and nobody replied. => Negative | It is a clear complaint.
- The shop opens at 9am. => Neutral | A fact with no feeling.
- Great phone, but the battery dies by noon. => Mixed | Praise and complaint together.
- Best customer service I have had in years! => Positive | Strong praise.
- The package weighs 2 kilograms. => Neutral | A plain fact.
- The app is beautiful, though sadly it crashes a lot. => Mixed | Good design, bad stability.
- I want my money back right now. => Negative | Clear anger and a demand.
- Delivery is free for orders above 10,000 naira. => Neutral | A policy statement.
- Love the design, hate the price. => Mixed | Both feelings clearly stated.
:::

### Tips

1. **Underline** the feeling words in your head ("great", "waited", "sadly").
2. Check whether there are **two** feelings, not one.
3. Do not add feelings the writer did not show.

::: check What makes a message Mixed?
+ A clear positive and a clear negative in the same message
- Any message with two sentences
- A fact
= Both feelings must be clear.
:::

## ❓ Drill 3: edge cases and "Unclear"

### Hard cases are normal

Real data is messy: sarcasm, one-word messages, slang, mixed languages. Good annotators:

1. Look for the guideline's **edge-case section**.
2. Choose the **most reasonable** reading.
3. Use the **Unclear** (or flag) option when there is **not enough information**, instead of guessing.

> 📌 **Remember:** "Unclear" is a valid answer. A forced guess is worse than an honest flag.

::: check A one-word message "Fine." with no context. Best label?
- Positive
- Negative
+ Unclear
= There is not enough to know the feeling.
:::

### The drill

::: drill Edge cases
labels: Positive | Negative | Unclear
rule: Use Positive or Negative only when the feeling is clear. Clear sarcasm counts as Negative. Use Unclear when the message could be read either way or has too little information.
pass: 75
- Oh great, another delay. Just what I needed. => Negative | Clear sarcasm, so Negative under this rule.
- Interesting. => Unclear | Too short to read any feeling.
- I love waiting in queues for three hours. => Negative | Obvious sarcasm.
- Not bad at all! => Positive | It means "good".
- Well, that happened. => Unclear | Could be said either way.
- Thanks a lot for nothing. => Negative | Clear sarcasm.
- Fine. => Unclear | No context.
- Absolutely brilliant service, will order again. => Positive | Clear, sincere praise.
:::

### Keep a notes file

Write down tricky cases and how you solved them. It makes you **faster and more consistent**, and shows reviewers a method.

::: check What should you do with tricky cases you solve?
+ Note them so you handle similar cases the same way
- Forget them
- Argue about them
= Consistency improves quality.
:::

## ✅ Drill 4: fact-checking and "Cannot verify"

Many AI projects ask: **is this statement correct?** Accuracy matters, because wrong facts in training data make AI worse.

### The three answers

- **Correct:** you are sure it is true.
- **Incorrect:** you are sure it is false.
- **Cannot verify:** it depends on information you do not have, or you cannot be sure.

> 📌 **Remember:** never guess. "Cannot verify" is honest, and a made-up answer is harmful.

::: check You are not sure whether a statement is true and cannot check. What do you choose?
- Correct
- Incorrect
+ Cannot verify
= Honesty about uncertainty is a skill.
:::

### How to check

1. Use **reliable sources** (official sites, trusted references).
2. Look for **two sources that agree**.
3. Watch for **recent changes**.
4. Do not rely on one random website.

### The drill

::: drill Fact check
labels: Correct | Incorrect | Cannot verify
rule: Mark Correct only if it is true. Mark Incorrect if it is false. Mark Cannot verify if it depends on private information you cannot check. Never guess.
pass: 75
- Abuja is the capital of Nigeria. => Correct | Abuja is the capital.
- A leap year has 365 days. => Incorrect | A leap year has 366 days.
- Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level. => Correct | That is the standard boiling point.
- Ada's phone number is 0801 234 5678. => Cannot verify | It is private information you cannot check.
- The Earth has two moons. => Incorrect | The Earth has one moon.
- There are seven days in a week. => Correct | True.
- My neighbour's shop made 2 million naira last year. => Cannot verify | You cannot check private figures.
- Lagos is the capital of Nigeria. => Incorrect | It is the largest city, but Abuja is the capital.
:::

## ⚖️ Drill 5: ranking AI answers

You will often see a question and two answers, and decide which is better, then **explain why**.

### The four checks, in order

1. **Correct:** are the facts right?
2. **Helpful:** does it answer what was asked?
3. **Clear:** is it easy to understand?
4. **Safe:** does it avoid harm and rude language?

> 📌 **Remember:** a **long answer is not better**. A short, correct, clear one beats a long, wrong one.

::: check Answer A is long but wrong. Answer B is short and correct. Which is better?
- A
+ B
- They are equal
= Correctness comes first.
:::

### Writing the reason

- **Weak:** "B is better."
- **Strong:** "B is better because it gives the correct date and answers the whole question. A has the wrong date."

### The drill

::: drill Ranking
labels: A is better | B is better | Equal
rule: Judge in this order: correct, helpful, clear, safe. Length does not make an answer better.
pass: 70
- Q: What is 12 x 12? A: 144. B: 124. => A is better | A is correct, B is wrong.
- Q: How do I boil an egg? A: Put the egg in boiling water for about 8 to 10 minutes, then cool it in cold water. B: Eggs are a nutritious food that many people enjoy in many different ways around the world. => A is better | A answers the question, B does not.
- Q: Is it safe to share my PIN? A: Yes, if the person says they are from the bank. B: No. Never share your PIN with anyone, even someone who says they are from the bank. => B is better | A gives unsafe advice.
- Q: What is the capital of France? A: Paris. B: The capital of France is Paris. => Equal | Both are correct and clear.
- Q: How many days are in March? A: 31. B: March has 30 days. => A is better | March has 31 days.
- Q: How can I save money? A: Track what you spend, set a goal and save part of your income first. B: Spend less. => A is better | A is more helpful and specific.
:::

### The honest-answer rule

Prefer the answer that **admits uncertainty** over one that **makes up facts**. A confident wrong answer is worse than a careful "I am not sure".

::: check Which is better when the true answer is unknown?
+ An answer that says it is not sure
- A confident invented answer
- A very long answer
= Honesty beats confident guessing.
:::

## ✍️ Drill 6: spelling and grammar review

Many tasks ask you to **fix text** or **choose the correct version**. Careful language skills are valuable.

### Common checks

- **Spelling:** *received*, not *recieved*.
- **Verb agreement:** *She does not like rice*, not *She don't*.
- **Its / it's:** *its* shows ownership, *it's* means *it is*.
- **Fewer / less:** *fewer* for things you count, *less* for amounts.
- **Their / there / they're.**

> 📌 **Remember:** read the whole sentence aloud in your head. Your ear often catches the error.

::: check Which is correct?
- It's tail is long.
+ Its tail is long.
- Its' tail is long.
= Its shows ownership; it's means it is.
:::

### The drill

::: drill Pick the correct version
labels: Version 1 | Version 2
rule: Choose the version with correct spelling and grammar.
pass: 75
- Version 1: I have went home. / Version 2: I have gone home. => Version 2 | "Have gone" is the correct form.
- Version 1: She doesn't like rice. / Version 2: She don't like rice. => Version 1 | "She doesn't" is correct.
- Version 1: Please send it to Ada and I. / Version 2: Please send it to Ada and me. => Version 2 | After "to" use "me".
- Version 1: There are fewer cars today. / Version 2: There is less cars today. => Version 1 | Cars can be counted, so "fewer".
- Version 1: Its raining outside. / Version 2: It's raining outside. => Version 2 | It's means it is.
- Version 1: We recieved your order. / Version 2: We received your order. => Version 2 | "Received" is the correct spelling.
- Version 1: Their going to the market. / Version 2: They are going to the market. => Version 2 | "Their" shows ownership.
:::

## 🎯 Quality, scores and common mistakes

### How quality is measured

- **Gold tasks:** hidden tasks with known answers (like the drills).
- **Reviews:** a senior reviewer checks a sample of your work.
- **Agreement:** how often your answers match other annotators.

> 📌 **Remember:** treat every task as if it will be checked, because some will be.

### The common mistakes

1. **Rushing:** skimming the instructions.
2. **Guessing:** choosing without understanding.
3. **Opinions over rules.**
4. **Copying AI answers** into tasks that must be your own work. This can get you removed.
5. **Inconsistency** between similar tasks.
6. **Working tired.**

::: check Which can get you removed from a project?
- Taking a short break
+ Pasting AI-generated text into a task that must be your own work
- Reading the guidelines twice
= Many projects ban AI-written answers when they ask for human judgment.
:::

### A quality routine

1. **Read the guideline** at the start of each session.
2. Work in **25-minute blocks**, then rest 5 minutes.
3. **Re-check** tricky items.
4. **Keep a notes file** of edge cases.
5. Review **feedback** and fix patterns.

### Accuracy first, then speed

```text
Accuracy first  →  consistency  →  then speed
```

Speed arrives naturally with practice. Rushing early lowers your score and can end your access.

::: check Which order is best for beginners?
- Speed, then accuracy
+ Accuracy, then consistency, then speed
- Neither matters
= Fast but wrong work costs you more than slow and right.
:::

## 🔎 Where jobs come from and how scams work

### Where real work comes from

- **AI and data companies** that hire annotators directly or through contractor programmes.
- **Marketplaces and platforms** that connect workers with projects.
- **Agencies** that supply workers for large projects.
- **Job boards**, such as the Career Forge **Jobs** page, which links to the employer's own application page.

> 📌 **Remember:** always apply on the **employer's own website**, never through a stranger's message.

::: check Where should you submit an application?
- To a stranger on WhatsApp who sends you a link
+ On the employer's own official website
- Anywhere that is fastest
= Check you are on the real site before entering any details.
:::

### Scam red flags

- They ask you to **pay** a fee, deposit or "training cost".
- They promise **guaranteed income** ("earn 50,000 a day").
- They contact you first on **WhatsApp or Telegram** and want to move off the platform.
- They send a **cheque** and ask you to send some back.
- They ask for your **bank PIN, password or OTP**.
- The email comes from a **free address** that does not match the company name.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** real companies never need your PIN, password or an OTP code.

### The drill

::: drill Scam triage
labels: Scam | Looks legitimate | Need to check
rule: Scam: asks for money, PINs or passwords, or promises guaranteed pay. Looks legitimate: no money asked, official channel, realistic claims. Need to check: could be real, but you must verify before trusting.
pass: 75
- Pay 7,500 naira for your training kit and we will send your login. => Scam | Real employers do not charge you to start.
- Apply on our careers page. We never charge applicants. => Looks legitimate | No money is asked and it uses an official page. Still check the web address.
- Congratulations! You earn 40,000 a day from your phone. Message me on Telegram. => Scam | Guaranteed high pay and an off-platform chat.
- Send your bank PIN to verify your account for payment. => Scam | No one legitimate needs your PIN.
- Your assessment starts Friday at 10am on the platform you applied through. => Need to check | Plausible, but confirm it came from the real company.

:::

## 💻 Setting up to work remotely

### Your setup

- **Device:** a laptop or desktop with a working keyboard and a decent screen.
- **Internet:** a reliable connection and a backup (mobile data).
- **Power:** a plan for outages: a charged laptop, a power bank or an inverter.
- **Space:** a quiet corner, even a small one.

> 📌 **Remember:** a stable setup protects your work. Plan for power and data before tasks begin.

::: check Why plan for power and internet cuts?
+ Lost connection mid-task can lose work or lower your score
- It makes the room look nicer
- It is only needed on weekends
= Reliability matters to employers.
:::

### Routine and focus

1. Work in **blocks** (25 minutes) with short breaks.
2. Keep **notes** of rules and tricky cases beside you.
3. **Silence notifications.**
4. **Stop when tired.** Mistakes cost more than breaks.

### Protecting yourself

- Use a **strong, different password** and **two-step verification**.
- Keep work accounts **separate** from personal ones.
- **Never share your login.** It can get you banned.
- Take care of your **eyes and posture**.

### Getting paid and keeping records

- Read **how and when** you are paid before you start.
- Keep a **record of tasks and payments**.
- Learn the basics of **tax** for freelancers in your country.
- **Do not depend on one project.** Work volume can change, so build income from more than one source.

::: check Why should you not rely on a single source of work?
+ Projects can end or slow down, so income can change
- Platforms are all bad
- It is against the rules to have more than one
= Variety protects you.
:::
